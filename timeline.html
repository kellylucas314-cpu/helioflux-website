First, the basics

What are biophotons?

Biophotons are extremely faint flashes of light that living cells give off on their own, with no outside light shining on them. The glow is a by-product of ordinary cell chemistry: as cells use oxygen to make energy, some of those reactions release a little of that energy as light.

Where it comes from, how faint it is, and why it matters

It is far too dim for the eye to see, but sensitive photon counters can measure it. Researchers call it ultraweak photon emission, or UPE. Because the light comes from the chemistry inside cells, its brightness, color and timing can shift when that chemistry changes. The century of research below traces how scientists learned to detect this light, and whether its patterns carry useful biological information.

Where it comes from
Reactions involving oxygen inside living cells, part of normal metabolism.
How faint it is
Far below what the eye can detect. Only extremely sensitive detectors, such as photon counters and cooled cameras, can register it.
Why it matters
When cell chemistry changes, the pattern of light can change with it. That is the signal researchers study.

1923

Historical hypothesis

A question begins with two onion roots

Gurwitsch reported more cell division on the side of one onion root facing another. He proposed that invisible light caused the effect, an early hypothesis based on cell behavior, not a direct measurement of light.

Why it mattersThe starting point for the historical research program that later developed into ultraweak-photon-emission studies.

Cell studyNIROSHA MURUGAN · First and corresponding author

Wavelength matters, not just brightness

Murugan’s team recorded cancer and non-cancer cell lines through six wavelength filters, one at a time. Raw brightness could not tell the groups apart, but light sorted by color did better.

Why it mattersTested spectral and temporal features as candidate indicators of cell state.

What the study showsFindings, context & original papersExplore studyClose details
The finding
Selected wavelength filters improved separation of cultured-cell groups.
Study context
Across all six filters, cancer lines leaned toward ultraviolet light and non-cancer lines toward infrared, a weak but opposite trend. With all six lines included, three of the filters (420, 620 and 950 nm) together classified 69% of recordings correctly. 2025

Human measurementNIROSHA MURUGAN · Senior, corresponding and lead-contact author

Recording faint light alongside human brain rhythms

Casey, Murugan and colleagues placed photon counters near the heads of 20 healthy adults and recorded light and EEG at the same time. In this early, exploratory study, light recorded near the head differed from background light.

Why it mattersExtends Murugan’s research from cell and animal models to simultaneous optical and electrical measurements in people.

What the study showsFindings, context & original papersExplore studyClose details
The finding
Reported structured photon-count dynamics and some relationships with simultaneously recorded EEG signals.
Study context
A proof-of-concept study with 20 participants, 18 of them in the EEG comparisons. Light recorded near the head differed from background light in its variability and in slow rhythms below 1 Hz. With eyes closed, the detector over the back of the head varied with EEG alpha rhythms, but so did a background detector pointed away from the head, so the authors call for experiments with electromagnetic shielding before firm conclusions. The authors describe the light as coming from the brain. The detectors sat about 5 cm from the scalp, so the setup cannot show which tissue produced it; the authors list the lack of readings from other parts of the body as a limitation. It is an early, exploratory step toward reading this light as a functional signal: a measurement milestone. The study itself did not involve cancer; HelioFlux sees it as a step toward human cancer studies.
Read the original
[30] Exploring ultraweak photon emissions as optical markers of brain activityOpen access.

What this evidence meansScience context +

Living cells give off light too faint for our eyes to see. Sensitive instruments can measure it, and experiments connect much of it to ordinary chemical reactions involving oxygen. Scientists call this ultraweak photon emission, or UPE. [Kobayashi 2009]

This timeline follows the evidence: early questions, direct measurements, images and studies of changing biological states. Measuring this light is established; using it to diagnose disease requires its own clinical evidence.

01

Established measurement

Biological photon emission can be detected and recorded under controlled conditions.

02

Active biological research

Researchers study how brightness, wavelengths and timing change with cell activity, stress and disease models.

03

Applications still to be validated

A measurable signal is not automatically a clinical test. Human cancer performance requires its own rigorous validation.

How to read this historyScope & evidence boundaries +

A curated history, not a census of every paper

This timeline selects historically influential work, direct measurements, mechanism studies and relevant newer directions through September 2026. Original reports take priority; historical reviews supply context where older full text is unavailable. Where a description rests on an abstract, a summary or a later review rather than the full paper, the source note says so. Inclusion is not a claim of consensus, replication or equal evidence strength.

Different kinds of light are not interchangeable

Spontaneous UPE is distinct from bright, specialized bioluminescence, externally excited fluorescence, light remaining after illumination (delayed luminescence), and infrared thermal radiation. Related methods are labeled where included. Detection alone does not demonstrate cell-to-cell communication, a coherent light field or quantum information processing.

Results belong to their experimental setting

Cell-line discrimination is not patient diagnostic accuracy. An animal group difference is not individual cancer detection. Healthy-human near-head photon measurements do not validate cancer screening.

Figures and illustrations

Original paper figures retain their panels, scales and authors’ credits. False colors encode measured intensity. Explanatory illustrations show the experiment or idea, with their scientific sources linked below; creation details are listed in the image credits. Restricted-rights figures are linked rather than reproduced.
