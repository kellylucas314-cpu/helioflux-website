01The biological signal

From cell chemistry to information

The pattern matters.
Not just the brightness.

Chemical reactions involving oxygen can release tiny amounts of light. Sensitive instruments let us measure it, even when our eyes cannot.

In small laboratory studies, cancerous and non-cancerous cell samples showed differences in their light-emission patterns. Researchers examine both the light’s color and how the signal changes over time. [1, 2]

Color and timing: what’s the difference?＋

Color describes the light’s wavelength. Timing describes how the measured signal varies over time. They are different ways of studying the same light.

The approximately 20 Hz feature discussed in the 2020 paper concerns changes over time, not the color of a photon.

What does “passive measurement” mean?＋

The proposed measurement collects light naturally emitted by tissue. It does not rely on excitation light, ionizing radiation, injected contrast, a blood draw or tissue removal. That describes the measurement approach, not a replacement for the tests used to diagnose cancer.

03Published research

A foundation in
published science.

Dr. Nirosha Murugan’s research spans laboratory cells, an animal cancer model and exploratory human measurement. Each answers a different question.

2018 Cell cultures

Can the light distinguish cell groups?

Selected wavelength bands helped distinguish cancerous and non-cancerous cell samples in a small laboratory study.

Study details & original paper＋

The study compared six cell lines. Its strongest quoted classification result, 92%, followed exclusion of the transformed HBL100 line. The result describes this study’s cell-sample analysis, not patient diagnostic performance.

Murugan et al. · Biophotonic markers of malignancy: Discriminating cancers using wavelength-specific biophotons. Biochemistry and Biophysics Reports.

2020 Cell cultures + mice

Do patterns change in a cancer model?

Cell-line analyses found distinguishing features. A separate mouse melanoma experiment found differences between groups before tumors were physically apparent.

Study details & original paper＋

The cell-culture analysis included 13 human and mouse cell lines. Selected signal-analysis settings yielded 90% discriminant accuracy.

The separate 47-mouse experiment included 23 mice given live melanoma cells, 8 given UV-killed cells and 16 uninjected controls. It found group-level signal differences as early as 24 hours. It did not report 90% animal diagnostic accuracy.

Murugan et al. · Ultraweak photon emissions as a non-invasive, early-malignancy detection tool: An in vitro and in vivo study. Cancers.

02
2025 Human measurement

What can we measure in people?

An exploratory study recorded faint light near the head alongside electrical brain activity in 20 healthy adults. This was measurement research, not a cancer study.

Study details & original paper＋

The study explored relationships between photon measurements and EEG. It did not establish where all recorded photons originated, and comparisons with nonbrain tissue remain important. These findings do not validate a cancer test or the current HelioFlux platform.

Casey et al. · Exploring ultraweak photon emissions as optical markers of brain activity. iScience. Nirosha Murugan, senior and corresponding author.

If cells have always given off this light, why is it only being used now?＋

Because it is astonishingly faint. The light sits near the floor of what any instrument can register, so for most of the last century the argument was whether it existed at all. Detectors that count single photons, and the computing needed to find patterns in those counts, only became practical in the last two decades.

Isn’t this just body heat, like thermal imaging?＋

No. Thermal imaging records infrared radiation associated with temperature. Ultraweak photon-emission research measures a much fainter signal associated with chemical processes. In the well-known 2009 human-imaging paper, the thermal image is a separate comparison, not the photon image.

Read the human-imaging paper ↗
How do you know the light comes from the cells and not the room or the instrument?＋

Recordings are made in darkened enclosures, and every detector has a known rate of false counts that is measured and accounted for. Samples are always compared against controls recorded the same way. The field learned this the hard way: some early claims did not survive careful re-testing, and those failures shaped the controls used today.

See the 1934 negative result →
What could this add that a scan or blood test cannot?＋

A readout of cell chemistry as it happens, taken from outside the body with nothing injected, drawn or irradiated. In laboratory and animal work, the light pattern shifted with malignancy before any tumor could be seen. Whether that holds in people, and whether it adds information a clinician can act on, is exactly what the planned human study is designed to test.

Would this replace a biopsy or a scan?＋

That is not established, and it is not the claim. The research question is whether photon measurements can add useful information before or alongside biopsy. Biopsy results would be the clinical reference for the planned feasibility work.

What would count as success in the first human study?＋

A measurable, repeatable difference between tissue that biopsy confirms as cancerous and tissue that it does not, using the same protocol on every participant. The goal is to show the signal carries useful information, not to make a diagnosis.
